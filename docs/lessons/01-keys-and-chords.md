# 01: Keys, scales and the chords inside them

**Status:** specified
**Builds on:** –
**Estimated sections:** 7

## Goal
Build the seven chords of any major or minor key, hear the difference between major,
minor and diminished triads, and understand why a key's chords have the qualities they do.

## Key concepts
- **Semitone (half step):** the distance between two neighboring keys on the piano.
- **Whole step:** two semitones.
- **Major scale:** seven notes following the pattern W W H W W W H.
- **Key:** a set of notes with one note that feels like home (the tonic).
- **Triad:** three notes built by stacking two thirds: root, third, fifth.
- **Major and minor third:** 4 and 3 semitones.
- **Chord quality:** major, minor or diminished, determined by the thirds a triad contains.
- **Diatonic chords:** the triads built only from the notes of a key.
- **Roman numerals:** chord names relative to the key (I, ii, vii°), independent of the actual key.
- **Relative minor:** the minor key sharing the same notes as a major key, starting on its 6th degree.

## Sections

### 1. The major scale
- **Hear:** the C major scale played up and down on the keys instrument, around 100 BPM.
- **See:** each note lights up on the piano as it plays; W and H labels appear between
  consecutive notes.
- **Read:** "Almost every song you know is built on a scale: seven notes out of the twelve
  on the piano. The major scale always follows the same pattern of whole steps and half
  steps: W W H W W W H. Start that pattern on any key and you get a major scale. The half
  steps are what give the scale its shape: they're the spots where notes lean towards
  their neighbors."
- **Try:** pick any tonic from a key selector (all 12, correctly spelled). The scale
  animates again from the new tonic with note names shown, so the user sees that the
  pattern stays the same while the black-key usage changes.

### 2. Building a triad
- **Hear:** C, then E, then G one by one, then all three together.
- **See:** on the scale C D E F G A B, the notes D and F fade out as "skipped", C, E and G
  stay highlighted with roles root, third and fifth in distinct colors. Interval labels
  show "major 3rd" (C–E) and "minor 3rd" (E–G).
- **Read:** "A chord is built by stacking every other note of the scale: take one, skip
  one, take one, skip one, take one. The result is a triad: a root, a third and a fifth.
  This stacking of thirds is the foundation of almost all Western harmony."
- **Try:** click any scale degree to build the triad starting there. It plays and shows
  root, third and fifth on the piano, with the interval labels.

### 3. The seven chords of a key
- **Hear:** all seven triads of C major played in order, one per beat.
- **See:** a row of seven chord cards (name, Roman numeral, notes). As each plays, its card
  and notes on the piano highlight. Major cards, minor cards and the diminished card use
  distinct visual treatments so the pattern is visible at a glance.
- **Read:** "Build a triad on each of the seven notes and you get the seven chords of the
  key. Some sound bright (major), some darker (minor), and one sounds tense and unstable
  (diminished). In every major key the pattern is the same: major on I, IV and V, minor on
  ii, iii and vi, diminished on vii°. That's why musicians talk in Roman numerals: a
  I–V–vi–IV progression sounds the same in any key."
- **Try:** click cards to play them in any order and build a four-chord loop by clicking
  four cards; the loop plays repeatedly with synced highlighting. Change the key and hear
  the same Roman numerals in a new key.

### 4. Why the qualities differ
- **Hear:** C–E played as a two-note interval, then D–F. Then C major and D minor triads.
- **See:** the semitones between the notes are counted on the piano: C to E is 4, D to F is 3.
  A small diagram shows each triad as its two stacked thirds:
  major = major 3rd + minor 3rd, minor = minor 3rd + major 3rd,
  diminished = minor 3rd + minor 3rd.
- **Read:** "The difference between major and minor is one semitone in the middle note.
  A major third is 4 semitones, a minor third is 3. Because the scale's steps are uneven
  (those two half steps), stacking thirds within it sometimes lands on a major third and
  sometimes on a minor one. The chord on vii° gets two minor thirds, which squeezes its
  outer notes to 6 semitones instead of 7. That narrowed fifth is what makes it sound tense."
- **Try:** a comparison tool: pick any two triads from the key and see their thirds
  counted side by side. A toggle raises or lowers the third of the selected chord by a
  semitone to hear major turn into minor and back.

### 5. Minor keys
- **Hear:** the loop C–F–G–C, then Am–F–G–Am. Same notes available, different home.
- **See:** the A natural minor scale on the piano uses exactly the same white keys as C
  major. Chord cards for A minor: i ii° III iv v VI VII.
- **Read:** "Every major key has a relative minor that shares all its notes. Start the
  C major scale on A instead of C and you get A natural minor: same notes, but a darker
  home. The chords are the same seven, just renumbered from the new tonic, so the pattern
  becomes i ii° III iv v VI VII. What makes something feel major or minor isn't the notes
  available, it's which chord feels like home."
- **Try:** a toggle switches between a major key and its relative minor, with the cards
  renumbering and the loops re-centering. The user picks a key and builds a loop in the
  minor version.

### 6. Experiment: sticking out
- **Hear:** C–Am–F–G in C major.
- **See:** all notes highlighted with the scale role.
- **Read (before):** "Every chord so far came from the key. What happens if we use one
  that doesn't?"
- **Try:** two swap buttons:
  - Swap F for **Fm**: the Ab highlights as outside-key. Explanation: "That Ab isn't in
    C major. The chord sounds bittersweet, as if the song briefly turned sad. It's
    borrowed from C minor, one of the most-used color moves in pop and film music."
  - Swap Am for **A major**: the C# highlights as outside-key. Explanation: "The C# wants
    to rise to D, so this chord pulls you forward towards Dm. Outside-key chords aren't
    wrong, they create color and direction. Later lessons are all about using them on
    purpose."
  The user can toggle each swap on and off while the loop plays.

## Genre connections
- **Pop:** I–V–vi–IV, all diatonic, used in countless songs.
- **Trance and EDM:** natural minor loops like i–VI–III–VII, all diatonic in minor.
- **Rock and folk:** I–IV–V as the backbone.
- **Classical and jazz:** vii° mostly acts as a stand-in for V; in pop and EDM it's rare
  as a plain triad.
