# 02: Harmonic function: home, away, tension

**Status:** built
**Builds on:** 01
**Estimated sections:** 8

## Goal
Hear the three roles chords play in a key (home, away, tension), know which chords belong
to each, and predict how a chord wants to move next.

## Key concepts
- **Function:** the role a chord plays in a key: home, away or tension.
- **Tonic:** the home chord (I), plus its stand-ins vi and iii.
- **Subdominant (predominant):** chords that move away from home: IV and ii.
- **Dominant:** chords that build tension towards home: V and vii°.
- **Function families:** chords in the same family share two of their three notes, so
  they can often stand in for each other.
- **Leading tone:** the 7th scale degree, a half step below the tonic, which wants to rise.
- **Tritone:** the 6-semitone interval inside vii°, which resolves inward by two half steps.
- **The usual flow:** tonic → subdominant → dominant → tonic, the path most progressions take.
- **Tension scale:** a rough number per chord for how far it is from home. A teaching
  model, not a law, so the lesson text can say "roughly".

## Sections

### 1. Home and away
- **Hear:** C–Am–F–G, then C–Am–F–G–C, two beats per chord at 90 BPM on the keys
  instrument. The first stops on G and hangs; the second comes home.
- **See:** two rows of chord cards, one per progression, labeled "Ends on V" and "Ends on
  I". As each chord plays, its card and its notes on the piano highlight. The last card of
  each row stays marked after playing, with the label "Hanging" or "Home".
- **Read:** "Play the chords of a key and one of them sounds like home: the tonic, I. The
  others all sound, more or less, like somewhere away from home. End a progression on I
  and it sounds finished. End it on V and it hangs in the air, waiting for more. That pull
  between home and away is what makes progressions move. The role a chord plays is called
  its function."
- **Try:** "Pick the last chord." The progression C–Am–F–_ with the seven chords of C
  major as cards below it. Clicking a card plays C–Am–F plus that chord. No right or
  wrong answer: the user listens for which endings sound finished (I, and more softly vi)
  and which hang (V, IV, ii). C major only.

### 2. The three families
- **Hear:** the seven chords of C major played family by family, one per beat with a beat
  of rest between families: C, Am, Em; F, Dm; G, Bdim.
- **See:** the seven chord cards in three labeled groups: "Tonic: home" (I, vi, iii),
  "Subdominant: away" (IV, ii) and "Dominant: tension" (V, vii°). Cards are colored by
  function and show the family name as text. Each stand-in card has a line such as
  "Shares C and E with I". Selecting a stand-in highlights the shared notes on the piano.
- **Read:** "The chords of a key fall into three families, by what they do. Tonic chords
  feel at rest: I, and its stand-ins vi and iii. Subdominant chords move away from home:
  IV and ii. Dominant chords build tension that wants to resolve home: V and vii°."
  Second paragraph: "Chords in a family share two of their three notes, which is why one
  can often replace another. iii is the odd one out: it's family of I, but it contains the
  leading tone, B, so it already leans a bit towards V."
- **Try:** the loop I–IV–V–I, with a choice under each of the first three chords to swap
  in a family member: I, vi or iii; IV or ii; V or vii°. The last chord stays I. A swap
  takes effect from the next chord while the loop plays, so the user hears that the
  progression still works: the same path, a different color. C major only.

### 3. Why V pulls
- **Hear:** G–C (V–I), then B–C alone, then Bdim–C (vii°–I). Two beats each at 80 BPM.
  Voicings: V as G3 B3 D4, vii° as B3 D4 F4, I as C4 E4 G4, so B3 rises to C4 and F4
  falls to E4. Other keys use the same shape: V and vii° just below the tonic, I from
  the tonic up.
- **See:** a zoomed-in two-voice view: the piano with brackets from B to C ("half step
  up") and from F to E ("half step down"), and a staff with two bars, the tritone B–F and
  its resolution C–E. The leading tone is labeled in the chord cards of V and vii°.
- **Read:** "V pulls home because of one note. Its third, B in C major, is the leading
  tone: a half step below the tonic, close enough that it wants to slide up into it. vii°
  has the same B, plus an F that leans down towards E. B and F are 6 semitones apart: the
  tritone, the most unstable interval in the key. When vii° moves to I, the tritone
  closes inward. B goes up to C, F goes down to E, and the tension is gone."
- **Try:** pick any major key; the leading tone and the tritone are respelled for that key
  (F# and C–F# in G major). Buttons: "Play V, then I", "Play vii°, then I", "Play the
  leading tone". Experiment toggle "Leave out the leading tone": V is played as root and
  fifth only (G3 D4) before I, so the user hears the pull weaken.

### 4. The usual flow
- **Hear:** I–IV–V–I, then I–ii–V–I, then I–vi–IV–V–I in C major, two beats per chord
  at 100 BPM.
- **See:** a flow diagram of three boxes in function colors, Tonic → Subdominant →
  Dominant, with an arrow from Dominant back to Tonic. As each chord plays, its family's
  box and its card light up. Below it, the seven chord cards colored by function.
- **Read:** "Many progressions take the same path: start at home, move away, build
  tension, come home. Tonic, subdominant, dominant, tonic. I–IV–V–I is the plainest
  version. Swap in family members and you get I–ii–V–I, I–vi–IV–V and countless others.
  Think of it as the grammar of harmony: not a rule, but the path most songs take."
- **Try:** build a four-chord loop by clicking cards (the loop builder from lesson 01).
  Slots show the numeral, the chord name and the function color. The loop starts playing
  when the fourth chord is picked, and the flow diagram follows along. A line under the
  loop reads its path, for example "T S D T: the usual flow" or "T D S T: from tension
  back to away, against the usual flow. Listen to how that feels." Pick any major key;
  a loop keeps its numerals when the key changes.

### 5. Seeing tension
- **Hear:** I–vi–IV–V twice, then I, in C major, two beats per chord at 100 BPM.
- **See:** a tension curve: one bar per chord, as high as its tension (0 to 4 in half
  steps) and colored by function, with the numeral underneath. The bar of the chord that
  is playing highlights, so the shape rises and falls with the music. The bar for iii
  carries a short label: "leans towards V".
- **Read:** "Give each chord a rough number for how far it is from home, and a
  progression gets a shape. Home, I, is 0. vi is 1, and iii is 1.5: it's family of I,
  but it contains the leading tone, so it already leans towards V. IV is 2 and ii is 2.5,
  since ii pushes harder towards V. V is 3, and vii°, with its tritone, is 4. I–vi–IV–V
  climbs step by step and falls back home when it starts again."
  Second paragraph: "These numbers are a teaching model, not a law. Real tension also
  depends on melody, rhythm and loudness. But the shape they draw is roughly what you
  hear."
- **Try:** choose a progression and see its curve before playing it: I–vi–IV–V,
  I–V–vi–IV, vi–IV–I–V, I–iii–IV–V and I–ii–V–I. Play loops the chosen progression; switching takes
  effect from the next chord. C major only.

### 6. Function in minor, a preview
- **Hear:** G–C (V–I in C major), then Em–Am (v–i in A minor), two beats each at 80 BPM.
- **See:** the seven chord cards of A minor colored by function: i, III and VI tonic;
  ii° and iv subdominant; v and VII dominant, both labeled "weak dominant". On the piano, brackets show the step to the
  tonic in each key: B to C "half step" in C major, G to A "whole step" in A minor.
- **Read:** "The families work in minor too: i, III and VI are home, ii° and iv move
  away, v and VII are the dominants. But natural minor has a catch. Its 7th note, G in
  A minor, is a whole step below the tonic: the subtonic from lesson 01. So v has no
  leading tone, and v–i sounds soft, more of a sigh than a pull. Composers fix this by
  changing one note, which is where lesson 07 picks up."
  Second paragraph: "VII does a similar job without a leading tone, which gives
  minor-key endings like VI–VII–i their own open sound. More on that in lesson 03."
- **Try:** pick any minor key. Buttons: "Play V–I in [relative major]" and "Play v–i in
  [minor key]". Clicking a card plays that chord.

### 7. Experiment: against the flow
- **Hear:** I–IV–V–I, then I–V–IV–I, in C major, two beats per chord at 100 BPM.
- **See:** the flow diagram from section 4, with the path of the playing progression
  drawn as arrows. In I–V–IV–I the step from Dominant to Subdominant is drawn as a
  backwards arrow.
- **Read (before):** "Function tells you what chords tend to do. What happens if we go
  against it?"
- **Try:** one toggle, "Swap IV and V", while the loop plays. Explanation shown when the
  swap is on: "V–IV runs backwards: the tension slides sideways instead of resolving, and
  IV–I brings you home more gently. It sounds looser and more relaxed, not wrong. It's
  the backbone of countless rock songs. Function explains tendencies, not laws."

## Quiz
Eight questions per attempt, generated from the theory engine so each attempt differs.
- **Ear (4 questions):** "Does this progression end at home or hanging?" A four-chord
  progression in a random major key plays, picked from patterns that follow the usual
  flow. Home: I–IV–V–I, I–ii–V–I, vi–IV–V–I. Hanging: I–vi–IV–V, I–IV–ii–V, I–iii–IV–V.
  At least one of each per attempt. Two options. Replay allowed.
- **Theory (4 questions),** one of each with random keys and degrees:
  - "What is the function of [numeral] in [key]?" Three options: tonic, subdominant,
    dominant.
  - "Which chord can stand in for [I, IV or V] in [key]?" Right: a family member (vi or
    iii for I, ii for IV, vii° for V). Wrong: chords from the other families.
  - "Which note is the leading tone in [key]?" Right: the 7th degree. Wrong: the tonic,
    the 6th degree and the 7th lowered by a half step (F in G major).
  - "Which chord is the dominant in [key]?" Right: V. Wrong: IV, vii° and I.
- Show the correct answer with a short explanation and a play button after each answer.
  For the ear questions the play button replays the progression followed by I, so a
  hanging ending gets resolved. Store the best score in storage.

## Genre connections
- **Pop:** I–V–vi–IV and vi–IV–I–V cycle through all three families; loops that end on V
  or IV keep the song moving into the next round.
- **Rock:** I–V–IV–I and V–IV–I run the flow backwards for a looser sound.
- **Classical:** tonic → subdominant → dominant → tonic as the norm, with ii and IV
  leading to V.
- **Jazz:** ii–V–I is subdominant, dominant, tonic in its purest form.
- **Trance and EDM:** minor loops like i–VI–III–VII often have no real dominant; tension
  comes from other devices (lessons 11 and 12).

## Components and theory functions
- **Existing components:** piano, chord card, chord board (cards, staff, piano), four-chord
  loop builder, key selector, choice group, play button, staff, quiz, sequence player.
- **New components:**
  - Function colors on chord cards and loop slots, with the family name as text so color
    is never the only cue. Three tokens per theme in css/tokens.css, distinct from the
    quality and role colors.
  - Flow diagram: Tonic → Subdominant → Dominant boxes with arrows; highlights the playing
    chord's family and can draw a progression's path, including backwards moves.
  - Tension curve (the "tension meter" in docs/curriculum.md): one bar per chord of a
    progression, height from `tension()`, with the playing bar highlighted.
- **New theory functions,** tests first:
  - `functionOf(degree, mode)` → `{ family, strength }`, with family "tonic",
    "subdominant" or "dominant" and strength "weak" for v and VII in minor, "normal"
    otherwise. The weak marker is data, not special-case code. Tests: C major I vi iii
    tonic, IV ii subdominant, V vii° dominant, all normal; A minor i III VI tonic, iv ii°
    subdominant, v VII dominant and weak.
  - `tension(degree, mode)` → I 0, vi 1, iii 1.5, IV 2, ii 2.5, V 3, vii° 4, for major
    only. Minor throws until lesson 07 defines it. Tests: every major degree, and minor
    throws.
  - `commonTones(chordA, chordB)` → shared note names, compared by spelling. Named so
    lesson 04 can reuse it. Tests: C and Am → C E; C and G → G; F and G → none.
  - `leadingTone(tonic)` → the 7th degree of the major scale. Tests: C → B, G → F#,
    F → E, Db → C.
  - `flowPath(degrees, mode)` → the functions in order, plus the positions of backwards
    moves (dominant → subdominant). Tests: I–IV–V–I has none; I–V–IV–I has one, from
    V to IV; I–vi–IV–V has none, including the loop back from V to I.
- **Architecture decisions:**
  - The sequence player already passes every field of a step back to `onStep`, so
    sections attach the degree (as the chord board does now) and look up function and
    tension with the theory functions. The audio module does not change.
  - `chord-board.js` and the voicing helpers in lesson 01's `shared.js` move to
    `js/lessons/common/`, since lesson 02 and later lessons use them. The loop builder stays
    an option of the chord board; section 4 is its first user. The board gets a
    `colorBy: "quality" | "function"` option; lesson 02 colors by function.

## Implementation decisions
Carried over from lesson 01:
- Nothing plays on arrival: each section starts with a Listen button, and the visual
  shows its finished state before listening.
- Chords use root position voicings with roots rising from the tonic, as in lesson 01,
  except the voicings given for section 3.
- Key selectors offer the same 12 major keys (and their relative minors) as lesson 01.
  Sections 3, 4 and 6 have one; the others stay in C major or A minor.

Decided while specifying:
- The tension scale uses half steps. iii (1.5) sits above vi (1): vi shares the tonic
  note with I, while iii contains the leading tone and leans towards V. ii (2.5) sits
  above IV (2): ii–V is the stronger push towards the dominant, while IV can go straight
  home (the plagal cadence in lesson 03).
- Minor keys keep the three families. v and VII are dominants marked weak, since neither
  has a leading tone. No fourth function.
- Minor tension values are left to lesson 07, where the major V in minor gives the scale
  something to compare against. Nothing in this lesson shows tension in minor, and a
  degree-based value would put ii° (B D F in A minor, the same tritone as vii° in major)
  well below vii°.

Decided while building sections 1–4:
- Section 1 colors its cards by quality, with degree names: functions are only named at
  the end of its read text and shown from section 2 on.
- Listen in sections 1 and 4 leaves two beats of rest between progressions. Section 2's
  Listen plays at 80 BPM with a beat of rest between families.
- Section 2: when a stand-in sounds, a bracket under the piano spans its two shared notes
  ("shared with I"); in a root-position triad they are always next to each other.
- Section 3 uses a three-octave piano like the other sections, so every key fits. At rest
  it shows the four notes of the resolution as scale notes with the two brackets; the
  staff noteheads are plain, not role-colored. V without its leading tone is colored as
  root and fifth.
- Section 4: a loop with no backwards move reads "the usual flow", also when it has no
  dominant (T S T T). The loop counts its move back to the first chord.

Decided while building sections 5–8:
- Section 5 shows only the curve and the piano, no cards. The curve shows the chosen
  progression; while Listen plays it shows I–vi–IV–V, then the chosen one again.
- Section 6 voices V and v just below their tonics, as section 3 does. The brackets run
  from the leading tone to the major tonic (above the keys) and from the subtonic to the
  minor tonic (below). The cards are the minor key's; V–I in the major key lights none.
- Section 7 draws the path as a strip under the flow diagram: the chords in order in
  function colors, with arrows between them and the backwards move marked "against the
  flow". Listen leaves two beats of rest between the progressions.
- Quiz: the ear questions' button reads "Play the progression". Sounds are stored as
  { tonic, degrees } and voiced like the lesson. A stand-in question's wrong options are
  three random chords from the other two families. After answering, a function question
  plays the chord, a stand-in question the main chord and then the stand-in, and the
  leading tone and dominant questions play V–I.
